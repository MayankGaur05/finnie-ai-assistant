# Beginner Financial Glossary

## Summary

This glossary explains selected personal-finance and investing terms in plain language. It is a short learning aid based on topics in Investopedia's financial dictionary, not a complete dictionary. The meaning of a term can depend on the product, market, or local rules.

## Terms

- **Asset:** Something a person or organization owns or controls that has economic value, such as cash, a bond, or a home.
- **Bond:** A debt security through which an investor lends money to an issuer, such as a company or government. The terms may specify interest payments and repayment of principal, but payment is not risk-free.
- **Budget:** A plan for expected income and spending over a period. It can help someone track expenses and plan savings.
- **Capital gain or loss:** The difference between an asset's sale proceeds and its cost basis. A sale above the cost basis generally creates a gain; a sale below it generally creates a loss. Fees and tax rules can affect the final result.
- **Diversification:** Spreading investments among different assets or holdings to reduce reliance on any one of them. It cannot eliminate risk or guarantee a profit.
- **Dividend:** A distribution a company may make to shareholders, often from profits or reserves. A company can change or stop dividends; they are not guaranteed.
- **Exchange-traded fund (ETF):** An investment fund whose shares trade on an exchange. An ETF holds assets according to its strategy, and its market price can differ from its net asset value.
- **Expense ratio:** A fund's annual operating expenses expressed as a percentage of its assets. These expenses reduce the fund's returns to investors.
- **Gold reserve:** A gold reserve is a fund of gold bullion or coin held by a government or bank.
- **Inflation:** A broad increase in prices over time, which reduces how much a given amount of money can buy.
- **Interest:** The cost of borrowing money or the amount paid for the use of money deposited or lent. The rate and calculation depend on the agreement or product.
- **Index:**  Weighted average of the prices or values of a sample of items chosen for their representativeness in relation to a whole.
- **Liquidity:** How quickly an asset can be converted to cash without a substantial change in its price. Liquidity can vary with market conditions.
- **Margin:** Margin in futures is a performance bond or "earnest money." Margin money is deposited by both buyers and sellers of futures contracts, as well as sellers of futures options.
- **Mutual fund:** A pooled investment vehicle that collects money from investors to buy a portfolio of assets. Investors own fund shares; the fund's value and risks depend on its holdings and terms.
- **Net asset value (NAV):** A fund's assets minus its liabilities, often expressed per share or unit. NAV is a valuation measure; it is not necessarily the price at which an exchange-traded fund can be bought or sold.
- **Portfolio:** The collection of investments or other financial assets held by a person or organization.
- **Return:** The gain or loss on an investment over a period, which may include changes in value and income such as interest or dividends. Return does not describe risk by itself.
- **Risk:** The possibility that an outcome differs from what is expected, including the possibility of losing some or all of the money invested. Different investments have different kinds and levels of risk.
- **Stock:** A security that generally represents an ownership interest in a company. Its price can rise or fall, and dividends, if any, are not guaranteed.
- **Yield:** Income from an investment expressed in relation to a price or amount invested, using a specified calculation. Different yield measures use different assumptions and may change over time.
- **Investment:** An asset or financial product acquired in the hope of income or growth. Its future result is uncertain and it can lose value.
- **Return on investment (ROI):** A ratio comparing a gain or loss with the amount invested. A simple ROI calculation may leave out timing, fees, taxes, and cash flows, so it does not tell the whole story on its own.

## Intermediate Terms

- **Annual percentage rate (APR):** A yearly rate that expresses the cost of borrowing, which may include interest and certain fees. What is included in APR can depend on the type of credit and applicable rules.
- **Asset allocation:** How an investment portfolio is divided among broad asset categories, such as stocks, bonds, and cash. An allocation's suitability depends on a person's circumstances; it does not guarantee a result.
- **Basis point:** One hundredth of a percentage point (0.01%). For example, 25 basis points equal 0.25 percentage points.
- **Bear market:** A period of substantial and often sustained decline in market prices. Definitions of the threshold and time period can differ.
- **Beta:** A measure comparing an investment's historical price movements with those of a reference market or index. It describes past co-movement under a particular calculation and does not predict future movement.
- **Bull market:** A period of rising market prices, often accompanied by positive investor sentiment. The term does not mean prices will keep rising.
- **Compound interest:** Interest calculated on an initial amount and on interest accumulated earlier. The actual result depends on the rate, compounding schedule, deposits, withdrawals, and fees.
- **Correlation:** A statistical measure of how two sets of values have moved in relation to each other over a period. Historical correlation can change and does not establish that one movement caused another.
- **Credit score:** A numerical estimate of credit risk produced using information in a credit report and a scoring model. Scores and their use vary by country, provider, and lender.
- **Current yield:** A bond's annual interest payment divided by its current market price. It does not include every possible source of return or loss, such as a price change before the bond is sold or matures.
- **Dollar-cost averaging:** Investing a set amount at regular intervals, regardless of the asset's price at each purchase. It changes the timing and size of purchases but cannot prevent losses or ensure a profit.
- **Duration:** A measure used to estimate how sensitive a bond's price may be to changes in interest rates. It is an estimate based on assumptions, not a guarantee of a bond's price movement.
- **Emergency fund:** Money kept accessible for unexpected expenses or interruptions in income. The amount and where to keep it depend on individual needs and circumstances.
- **Index:** A calculated measure that tracks the performance of a selected group of securities or other assets. An index itself is generally a benchmark; an investment product may seek to track it.
- **Limit order:** An instruction to buy or sell a security only at a specified price or better. It may not execute if the market does not reach the limit price.
- **Market capitalization:** The market value of a company's outstanding shares, commonly calculated as share price multiplied by shares outstanding. It can change as the share price or share count changes.
- **Price-to-earnings ratio (P/E):** A ratio comparing a company's share price with its earnings per share, using a specified historical or estimated earnings figure. It is one measure and should not be interpreted without context.
- **Real rate of return:** An investment return adjusted for inflation, giving an estimate of the change in purchasing power. Fees, taxes, and the chosen inflation measure can also affect an investor's actual outcome.
- **Standard deviation:** A statistical measure of how widely values have varied around their average over a period. In investing it is sometimes used as a measure of historical volatility, but it does not capture every kind of risk.
- **Yield to maturity (YTM):** An estimate of a bond's annualized return if held to maturity, based on its current price, payments, and principal repayment assumptions. It assumes scheduled payments occur and the bond is held to maturity; actual results can differ.

## Expert Terms

- **Alpha:** A measure of an investment's return relative to a benchmark or model's expected return, under a stated methodology. Different methods can produce different alpha estimates, and historical alpha does not establish future performance.
- **Arbitrage:** A strategy that seeks to profit from price differences for equivalent or closely related assets in different markets. Apparent opportunities may disappear quickly and can involve execution, financing, and other risks.
- **Capital asset pricing model (CAPM):** A model that estimates an asset's expected return using a risk-free rate, its beta, and an expected market risk premium. Its result depends on assumptions and inputs; it is not a forecast guarantee.
- **Capital gains yield:** The portion of an investment's return attributable to a change in its price, usually expressed as a percentage of the starting price over a stated period. It excludes income such as dividends or interest.
- **Convexity:** A measure of how a bond's duration may change as its yield changes. It can help describe the curvature in the estimated relationship between bond prices and yields; it does not remove interest-rate risk.
- **Counterparty risk:** The possibility that the other party to a contract or transaction will not meet its obligations. The exposure depends on the arrangement, collateral, and protections in place.
- **Credit spread:** The difference between yields on debt instruments with different credit or other characteristics, often measured against a government benchmark. It can reflect several factors and is not a pure measure of default risk.
- **Derivative:** A financial contract whose value is linked to an underlying asset, rate, index, or event. Derivatives vary widely and can involve leverage, complex obligations, and the risk of losses greater than an initial payment.
- **Discounted cash flow (DCF):** A valuation method that estimates an asset's value by converting projected future cash flows into present value using a discount rate. Its output is sensitive to assumptions about cash flows, timing, and the discount rate.
- **Efficient frontier:** In modern portfolio theory, the set of portfolios estimated to offer the highest expected return for each level of expected risk, or the lowest expected risk for each expected return, given specified inputs. It relies on estimates and assumptions that may not hold.
- **Equity risk premium:** The expected return on equities above a selected risk-free rate. It is an estimate that depends on the chosen market, period, and methodology, not a guaranteed extra return.
- **Free cash flow (FCF):** Cash generated by a business after certain operating costs and capital expenditures, as defined by the calculation used. Definitions can vary, and FCF alone does not show a company's full financial condition.
- **Information ratio:** A measure comparing a portfolio's active return over a benchmark with the variability of that active return, often called tracking error. Results depend on the period, benchmark, and calculation choices.
- **Jensen's alpha:** A portfolio-performance measure that compares actual return with the return predicted by the CAPM for the portfolio's beta. It relies on the model, benchmark, and selected time period.
- **Modified duration:** An estimate of a bond's percentage price sensitivity to a change in yield, under a small-change approximation. It is less reliable for large yield changes or bonds with embedded options unless adjustments are made.
- **Monte Carlo simulation:** A modeling technique that runs many scenarios using randomized inputs to estimate a range of possible outcomes. Its results depend on assumptions and input distributions and do not predict what will happen.
- **Sharpe ratio:** A measure of historical excess return per unit of measured volatility, using a selected risk-free rate and time period. It does not capture every type of risk and is not a standalone prediction of future performance.
- **Tracking error:** A measure of how much a portfolio's returns have varied relative to a benchmark over a period. A low value indicates closer historical tracking, not that the portfolio is risk-free or will keep tracking closely.
- **Value at risk (VaR):** A model-based estimate of a loss threshold for a specified portfolio, confidence level, and time horizon. VaR does not state the maximum possible loss and may understate rare or extreme outcomes.
- **Weighted average cost of capital (WACC):** An estimate of a company's average financing cost, weighted across sources such as equity and debt, often used as a discount rate in valuation. Its inputs are estimates and the appropriate rate depends on the cash flows being evaluated.

## Additional Terms

- **Accrued interest:** Interest that has built up on a loan or bond since the last payment date but has not yet been paid. In a bond transaction, the buyer may compensate the seller for accrued interest, depending on the settlement terms.
- **Amortization:** The scheduled repayment of a loan over time through payments that cover interest and reduce principal. The split between interest and principal can change over the repayment period.
- **Basis risk:** The possibility that a hedge and the exposure it is intended to offset will not move together as expected. This can leave some risk even when a hedge is in place.
- **Bid-ask spread:** The difference between the highest price a buyer is currently offering (bid) and the lowest price a seller is currently accepting (ask). It is one trading cost and can widen when liquidity is low or markets are unsettled.
- **Default risk:** The possibility that a borrower will fail to make required interest or principal payments. Credit ratings and other analyses estimate risk but cannot guarantee repayment.
- **Forward contract:** A customized agreement between parties to buy or sell an asset at a specified price on a future date. It can expose each party to counterparty and market risks.
- **Futures contract:** A standardized exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specified future date and price. Futures can involve leverage and daily settlement; losses can be substantial.
- **Hedging:** Taking a position intended to offset some exposure to a financial risk. A hedge can reduce one risk while adding costs or other risks, and may not work as intended.
- **Liquidity premium:** The additional return investors may require for holding an asset that is harder to buy or sell quickly at a predictable price. The premium is uncertain and can change with market conditions.
- **Margin:** Money or eligible assets deposited with a broker or other intermediary to support a position. Trading on margin can magnify gains and losses, and may lead to a forced sale or a request for additional funds.
- **Nominal return:** An investment's return before adjusting for inflation. It may also exclude fees and taxes unless those are included in the calculation.
- **Option:** A contract giving its buyer the right, but generally not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at specified terms before or at an expiration date. Options can expire worthless and involve complex risks.
- **Rebalancing:** Adjusting a portfolio's holdings to bring its allocations closer to chosen target weights. Rebalancing can create transaction costs or tax consequences and does not ensure better performance.
- **Short selling:** Selling a borrowed security with the aim of buying it back later at a lower price. If its price rises, losses can be large and may exceed the original amount received.
- **Systematic risk:** Risk that affects a broad market or many investments and cannot generally be removed just by holding more securities. Examples can include economy-wide or interest-rate changes.
- **Tax-loss harvesting:** Selling an investment at a loss to realize that loss for potential tax treatment, often while changing the portfolio exposure. Tax rules and restrictions vary by jurisdiction, so the result depends on an individual's circumstances.
- **Tracking difference:** The gap between an investment fund's return and the return of the index or benchmark it seeks to follow over a stated period. Fees, trading, and portfolio construction can contribute to the difference.
- **Unsystematic risk:** Risk specific to a company, issuer, or industry. Holding a wider range of investments may reduce this type of exposure, but does not eliminate market-wide risks.
- **Yield curve:** A graph showing yields for debt securities with similar credit features but different maturities. Its shape can change and does not by itself predict economic or market outcomes.
- **Zero-coupon bond:** A bond that does not make regular interest payments and is generally issued at a discount to the amount due at maturity. Its price can fluctuate, and repayment depends on the issuer meeting its obligation.

## Limitation

These are simplified educational descriptions, not individualized financial, investment, tax, or legal advice. Definitions can vary by jurisdiction and product documents. Investments can lose value; no term or metric guarantees a particular outcome.
