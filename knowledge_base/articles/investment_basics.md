# Investment Basics

## Summary

An investment is an asset purchased with the expectation that it may produce income, increase in value, or both over time. Investing uses resources today, usually money, in the hope of a future benefit. The outcome is uncertain, and an investment can lose value.

- Common investment types include stocks, bonds, mutual funds, exchange-traded funds (ETFs), and real estate. Each has different features, costs, risks, and ways of generating returns.
- Returns may come from income, such as interest, dividends, or rent, and from an increase in an asset's value. A price increase becomes a realized gain only when the asset is sold for more than its cost; selling for less can create a loss.
- Before investing, consider whether near-term expenses and unexpected costs are covered. Also understand what an investment holds, how easily it can be sold, its fees, and the possibility of loss.
- Diversification means spreading money across investments that may respond differently to events. It can reduce exposure to a single investment, but cannot guarantee a profit or prevent losses.
- Return on investment (ROI) is one way to express gain or loss relative to the original amount invested: `(current value - original value) / original value`. A simple ROI figure may not account for the time invested, additional deposits or withdrawals, fees, taxes, or income received, so it may not be enough by itself to compare investments.
- Risk and potential return are related considerations, but a higher expected return is not guaranteed compensation for taking more risk. Consider goals, time horizon, ability to withstand losses, and personal circumstances.

## Limitation

This article is a general educational summary, not individualized financial, tax, or investment advice. Investment products, account rules, taxes, and investor protections vary by jurisdiction. Past or expected returns do not guarantee future results.
