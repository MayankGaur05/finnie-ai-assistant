# Mutual Funds and Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)

## Summary

Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) pool money from many investors to invest in a collection of assets, such as stocks or bonds. Each investor owns shares of the fund rather than directly owning every investment in its portfolio.

- Both types of funds can make it easier to hold a range of investments. However, a fund is not automatically diversified: a narrowly focused fund may concentrate on one industry, and some ETFs track a single stock.
- Mutual fund shares are generally bought from or redeemed with the fund, directly or through a financial intermediary. Orders are typically completed at the fund's next calculated net asset value (NAV), which is the per-share value of its assets minus liabilities.
- ETF shares are bought and sold on an exchange through a broker while the market is open. Their trading price can differ from the ETF's NAV, and investors may pay a bid-ask spread or broker transaction costs.
- Both fund types have fees and expenses. These costs reduce investment returns, so investors can review a fund's prospectus and fee table to understand its costs.
- Tax treatment can depend on the account, the fund, and the investor's circumstances. In a taxable account, either type of fund may make taxable capital-gains distributions. Tax rules differ by country and account type.

## Limitation

Mutual funds and ETFs can lose value, and diversification does not guarantee a profit or prevent losses. This article explains general features and trade-offs; it does not recommend a particular fund or provide individualized financial, tax, or investment advice. Rules and tax treatment vary by jurisdiction.
