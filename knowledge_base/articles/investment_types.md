# Common Types of Investments

## Summary

Investments are assets or financial products people use in pursuit of future income or growth. Each type works differently and carries its own risks, costs, liquidity, and potential return. No investment type guarantees a gain.

- **Stocks:** A stock represents partial ownership in a company. Investors may benefit from price increases or dividends, but the price can fall and dividends are not guaranteed.
- **Bonds:** A bond is a loan to a government, municipality, or company. The issuer may pay interest and repay principal at maturity according to the bond's terms. Bonds can lose value, and issuers may fail to make payments.
- **Mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs):** These funds pool investor money to hold a collection of assets. Mutual funds are typically priced once at the end of each trading day; ETFs trade on an exchange during the day. A fund's holdings, strategy, costs, and risks vary, and a fund is not automatically diversified.
- **Certificates of deposit (CDs) and savings products:** These are deposit products offered by financial institutions, with terms and returns that depend on the provider and account. Withdrawal restrictions, inflation, and local deposit-protection rules may matter. They are different from market-traded securities.
- **Real estate and real estate investment trusts (REITs):** Direct property ownership may produce rent or a change in property value, but can involve maintenance costs, vacancies, and difficulty selling quickly. REITs provide exposure to real-estate-related assets through a company or fund structure; their shares or units can still fluctuate in value.
- **Commodities:** These include raw materials such as metals, energy products, and agricultural goods. Exposure may be direct or through financial products. Prices can be volatile and may be affected by supply, demand, weather, and geopolitical events.
- **Alternative investments and derivatives:** This broad category can include private equity, hedge funds, options, and futures. Products differ widely and may involve leverage, limited liquidity, complex terms, or substantial risk. They can be difficult to understand and may not be suitable for every investor.

## Comparing investment types

When learning about an investment, consider what it represents, how it may generate a return, what it costs, how readily it can be sold, and what could cause a loss. Risk varies within each category; a label such as "stock," "bond," or "fund" alone does not show how risky a particular investment is. Diversification can spread exposure across different investments, but it cannot eliminate risk or guarantee a profit.

## Limitation

This article is a general educational summary, not individualized financial, tax, or investment advice or a recommendation to buy any product. Investment products, terminology, investor protections, and tax rules vary by jurisdiction. Check the offering documents and local rules for a specific product.
